# SUN 796-Bus Interface Manual


<center>SUN Microsystems Inc.</center>


<center>June 1982</center>


**Abstract**

The SUN Workstation uses the IEEE 796-Bus (Intel Multibus) internally.
this document describes design considerations when interfacing
other 796-Bus boards to SUN 796-Bus Boards, in particular to the
SUN 68000-10 Board.


**Installation Considerations**

When installing boards into the SUN Workstation cardcage, the following
rules should be ovserved:

`POWER`: Make sure that the additional boards do not exceed the
capabilities of the built-in power supply.
The standard SUN power supply provides 20A at 5V,
3A at -12V, 5A at +12V, and 3.5A at -5A.

`COOLING`: The cooling system in the SUN is designed to ensure
that the internal operating temperature does not exceed 130 degree
F or 55 degree C with an ambient temperature of 100 degree F or 37 C.
The top slot of the cardcage receives less cooling than lower slots.
Do not use the top slot for boards dissipating more than 15 Watts.

`CAUTION`: To prevent possible equipment damage,
do not install board in a cardcage while power is on.
Also, to prevent damage due to static voltages,
avoid exposing the board to plastic materials.

**Overview**

The IEEE-796 Bus is an asynchronous bus, accommodating devices with
various transfer rates while maintaining maximum throughput.
The IEE-796 Bus provides 8-bit and 16-bit data transfers
and 20-bit addressing with optional 24-bit addressing.
The Multibus allows multiple masters to share the same bus,
arbitrating bus mastership via serial (daisy-chain) or parallel priority
resolution schemes.

**History**

The Multibus (TM Intel Corporation) was developed by Intel Corporation
in 1974 for use in the microprocessor development systems
and board-level products. The original design with 8-bit data and 16-bit
address busses was refined in 1977 to allow for 8-bit or 16-bit data transfers,
20-bit addressing, and enhanced Multimaster capabilities.
In March 1978, the IEEE Computer Standards Committee took up the
Intel Multibus in an effort to make it a multivendor industry standard.
The proposed IEEE 796-bus standard refined several timing specifications
and made other extensions to the Intel Multibus, such as a 24-bit address
extension on the P2-Bus.

Intel Corporation has applied for a patent regarding
the byte-swapping and related mechanism of the Multibus.
At the time of this writing, the patent application is still pending.
Intel Corporation has announced that in the case a patent were granted,
patent licensees will be available to anyone for a nominal, low-cost fee.

**Rational**

The interconnecting bus structure of a computer system is one
of the most important elements in of the overall design, because the
bus defines the interface and communication capabilities
of all system components the bus interconnects.

The SUN Workstation uses two busses: a synchronous, high-speed memory bus
for processor-memory communication and the IEEE 796 Bus
or Intel Multibus for input/output and pheripherals.
The IEEE 796 Bus was chosen for the following main reasons:


Popularity: The Multibus is one of the most popular microprocessor
busses. A wide number of board level components are available for it,
both low-cost and high-performance.

Packaging: The Multibus offers a convenient form factor for
packaging a microcomputer-based system. The 6" by 12" boards
can hold more than 100 integrated circuit packages.
Off-the shelf packaging and prototyping boards are available.

Performance: The Multibus matches well with state-of-the-art,
high-performance 16-bit microprocessors such as the 68000/68010.
The Multibus bandwidth allows high-perfomance input/output.

P2-Connector: The Multibus has a secondary backplane connector,
the P2 connector, reserved for intermodule connections.
This connector is used in the SUN Workstation for the memory bus.

Non-Proprietary: An important consideration was that the Multibus is
a multi-vendor, non-proprietary bus, which has been reviewed
by an independent standard organization leading up
to the current IEEE-796 standard.

**The SUN-68000 Board as a Multibus Master**

The SUN-68000 Board has a number of characteristics that
need to be observed to successfully interface it to other Multibus boards.

*Data Transfers*.
The SUN-68000 Board allows word and byte transfers via the Multibus.

*Byte Order*. In order to offer a consistent model for 68000 programmers,
the SUN-68000 board generates 68000 byte order on the Multibus.
This means that the low-order or the even byte is placed in D8 through D15,
whereas the high-order or odd byte is placed in bits D0 through D7.
If the SUN-68000 board communicates with a byte-organized Multibus device,
it is typically necessary to reverse the byte-order in software.

*Addressing*.
The SUN-68000 Board generates the 20 address lines on the standard IEEE-796 Bus
Using the 20 address lines of the standard IEEE-796 Bus, the board can address up
to 1 Megabyte of memory and 1 Megabyte of input/output locations.

*Multimaster Operation*.
The SUN 68000 board has full multi-master capabilities that allow it to
share the 796-Bus with other processor boards or DMA devices.  The
on-board arbitration logic automatically arbitrates access to the bus when an
off-board cycle is executed, yielding to higher priority bus masters.  Up to
three masters can be in a system utilizing the serial priority method, or up to
16 masters in a system with parallel priority arbitration.
Note that all on-board devices are private to the board and cannot
be accessed from the Multibus.

*Clock Generation*.
The Multibus defines two clocks, Bus Clock (BCLK) and Constant Clock (CCLK).
Bus Clock is used for the multimaster arbitration logic. Constant Clock
provides a centralized clock source to generate timing delays on
pheripheral boards. The bus standard specifies both clocks to be 10 Mhz or less
and that they are driven by a single busmaster only.

The SUN 68000 board is factory wired to provide the bus clock
and constant clock signals at a frequency of 9.8304 MHz.
If the SUN 68000 board is used in a system where another
board provides the bus clock or the constant clock,
the respective clock needs to be disabled.
Bus clock is disabled by cutting jumper J901-5:6.
Constant clock is disabled by cutting jumper J903-1:2.

*Init Generation*.
The SUN 68000 Board contains an on-board precision voltage reference
for power-on reset.
Multibus INIT is generated whenever the voltage falls below 4.5 Volt.
In addition, Multibus INIT can be generated by executing the
68000 RESET instruction.
Note that in its standard configuration the SUN 68000 board always drives
INIT to the Multibus and that the SUN 68000 board cannot be reset by an
INIT from the Multibus.


*Bus Timeout*.
If the 68000 accesses the Multibus and does not receive an
data transfer acknowledge within 1 to 3 millisecond, the access
will be aborted via bus error.
The timeout period in the SUN-68000-10 Board includes the
Multibus aquisition time. Thus, if a pheripheral device locks
up the bus for more than 1 milliseconds, timeouts can occur.


#### Interrupts


To understand how the 68000 Board processes Multibus interrupts,
it is necessary to first review the 68000 interrupt mechanism.

The 68000 has seven interrupt levels, numbered 1 through 7, with level 7 being
the highest priority and level 1 the least priority.  Interrupts are recognized
for all priority levels greater than the current processor priority contained
in the 68000 status register.  When an interrupt is acknowledged, the processor
priority is set to the level of the interrupt request.

A level 7 interrupt is special in that it is recognized even if the mask in the
68000's status register is set to 7, thus providing a non-maskable interrupt
capability.  A level 7 interrupt is acknowledged every time the interrupt
request changes from a lower level to level 7, that is, level 7 interrupts are
"edge-triggered".

The 796-Bus standard defines 8 interrupt lines, INT0 through INT7, with INT0
being the highest priority.  Also, the standard recommends that interrupts be
level-triggered instead of edge-triggered to allow multiple interrupt sources
on each interrupt line.
In addition, the Multibus provides both vectored and non-vectored interrupts.

The 796-Bus interrupt definitions have been mapped by the SUN 68000 Board
as follows:
To avoid confusion for 68000 programmers, the numbering and the priorities of
the interrupt lines on the 796-Bus was made corresponding to the definition of
the 68000.  Thus INT7 on the 796-Bus is the highest priority interrupt and INT1
the lowest.  INT0 is not implemented.  In addition, INT7 is non-maskable and
edge-triggered, whereas all other interrupts are maskable and level-triggered.

Three interrupt lines are assigned to on-board interrupt sources:
`INT7:` Refresh timer, `INT6:` User Timer, `INT5:` UART.
These three interrupt lines are normally not connected to the Multibus,
leaving interrupt levels one, two, three and four for the Multibus.
Jumpers are provided if alternate interrupt assignments are desired.

Interrupts are acknowledged by the 68000 in auto-vector mode, that is, the
interrupt vector is generated internally by the 68000 and is not supplied by
the device.  Thus the INTA signal on the 796 Bus and the interrupt vector
capabilities of the 796-Bus are not used.
However, the 68000 can quickly determine in software the nature of the
vectored interrupt, as long as the vector information can be read back.
Designing with non-vectored interrupts reduces chipcount
and thus manufacturing costs. In addition, interrupt vectors
are typically specific to particular CPUs and/or pheripheral devices.
Thus non-vectored designs are more general and flexible.


**Design of Multibus Slave Boards for the SUN Workstation**

Attention is called to the loading rules regarding inputs and
the driving rules for outputs (Table B3 in the Intel Specification).
The following is a brief summary of design recommendations.

**Inputs:**
Data and address inputs should be at most two low-power
Schottky (LS) loads, control line inputs no more than 1 Schottky (S) load.
It is highly recommended to use inputs with Schmitt-Trigger (hysteresis)
to enhance noise immunity, especially for the control lines.

**Outputs:**
Data outputs should be driven by standard bus transceivers, e.g. 8303B.
XACK should be driven by a 74S240 type device.
Notice that XACK\ both needs to be driven inactive (high) as well
as TRI-STATE when disabled (unfortunately the Bus Standard does
not specify what the disable time should be).
It is standard practice to use the same signal for deasserting XACK
as well as tristating the driver, because signal propagation times
are typically shorter than tristating times.
